# IQO chemistry notes (retrieval corpus)

Reading a burette: read the bottom of the concave meniscus with your eye level to the liquid surface, using a white card behind the burette. Record to two decimal places, for example 12.35 mL, and read the initial and final volumes the same way.

Burette preparation: rinse the burette with a little of the titrant, fill it, open the tap to remove any air bubble from the tip, and record the starting volume before the first addition.

Phenolphthalein is colourless below about pH 8.2 and pink to magenta by pH 10.0. It is the usual indicator for strong acid with strong base, and also suits weak acid with strong base because the equivalence point is above pH 7.

Methyl orange changes from red to yellow between about pH 3.1 and 4.4. It suits strong acid with weak base titrations, where the equivalence point is below pH 7.

Overshot endpoint: if the solution turns dark magenta the endpoint has been passed. Record the overshot volume, discard the flask contents into the waste carboy, rinse, and repeat the titration adding titrant dropwise near the end. The faint pale pink that lasts about thirty seconds is the correct endpoint.

Titration calculation: at the endpoint for a one to one reaction such as HCl with NaOH, C acid times V acid equals C base times V base. Moles equal concentration in mol per litre times volume in litres, and moles also equal mass divided by molar mass.

Molar masses: NaOH 40.00 g/mol, HCl 36.46 g/mol, H2SO4 98.08 g/mol, acetic acid 60.05 g/mol, NaCl 58.44 g/mol.

Dilution rule: always add acid slowly to water while stirring, never water to acid. Concentrated sulfuric acid is about 98 percent and releases a large amount of heat when diluted, which can make water boil and spit. Handle concentrated acids in the fume hood with goggles, nitrile gloves and a lab coat. For dilution use C1 V1 equals C2 V2.

Hydronium and sulfate: sulfuric acid is a strong acid in its first dissociation, and hydrogen sulfate has a second pKa of about 2. In dilute solution hydronium and sulfate ions are simply the dissociated form of sulfuric acid, a conjugate acid base system in equilibrium, and not hazardous on their own.

Bleach with ammonia forms toxic chloramine gases. Bleach with acids, including vinegar or hydrochloric acid, releases chlorine gas. Both require immediate evacuation to fresh air.

Cyanide salts with acid release hydrogen cyanide gas, which is lethal. Concentrated nitric acid is a strong oxidiser and can react violently or explosively with organic solvents such as acetone or alcohols.

Sodium and potassium metal react with water to give hydrogen gas and heat, and can ignite. They are stored under oil, and fires need a Class D extinguisher or dry sand, never water.

Chemical splash on skin or in the eyes: go to the eyewash or safety shower, flush continuously for fifteen minutes, remove contaminated clothing, and get medical help.

GHS pictograms: GHS01 exploding bomb is explosive, GHS02 flame is flammable, GHS05 corrosion causes burns and eye damage, GHS06 skull and crossbones is acutely toxic, GHS07 exclamation mark is irritant or harmful, GHS09 environment is hazardous to aquatic life.

PPE for a teaching lab: chemical splash goggles, a lab coat, nitrile gloves, long trousers and closed shoes. Nitrile gloves do not protect against every solvent, so check the safety data sheet.

Waste segregation: keep acid waste, base waste, halogenated organics, non-halogenated organics and heavy metal waste in separate labelled carboys. Never mix waste streams and do not pour chemicals down the sink unless the instructor confirms it is allowed.

Making a standard solution: weigh the solute, dissolve it in less than the final volume of water in a beaker, transfer to a volumetric flask rinsing the beaker into it, then make up to the mark with the bottom of the meniscus on the line and invert to mix.
